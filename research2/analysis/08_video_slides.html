This is a controlled study of visual imitation learning on a Panda manipulation benchmark. The driving question: when demonstration collection is expensive, which visual diversity should we collect first? We compare three model families across four visual axes and a hundred forty-four datasets, and the headline is sharper than just "diversity helps."

Transcript · target 45s

The human-robot interaction component is the data collection decision: a human chooses what demonstrations the robot sees, and that choice determines whether the robot acts robustly. Pixel behavior cloning is appealing because it’s simple, but it’s brittle: small errors compound under closed-loop control. Existing large datasets like BridgeData and MimicLabs scale up by varying many factors at once, so it’s hard to know which kind of diversity actually drove generalization. R3M and similar work argue pretrained encoders help. DAgger formalized the covariate shift we run into in closed loop.

Transcript · target 50s

The training objective is the behavior cloning loss from Lecture 8: minimize one-over-N times the sum of squared errors between the expert action a-sub-h and the policy output pi-theta of s, over the expert dataset D. The closed-loop dynamics from the MDP framing in Lecture 9 explain why errors compound: once the robot acts, its own action shifts the next state. So we evaluate closed-loop rollouts, not just training loss. For success we compute whether the end effector ever gets within an epsilon of the target — at four thresholds from half a centimeter to five — and we also report the final distance, because a policy can pass near the target and then drift away.

Experimental setup

V‑BCOOD: Two Tasks, Four Visual Axes, 144 Datasets

  • Tasks: direct reach · obstacle‑aware reach (route around blocking obstacle).
  • Visual axes: color, spatial distribution, camera viewpoint, lighting direction&intensity.
  • 16 train configs = each axis × narrow/diverse × 2 tasks.
  • Budgets: 5 / 20 / 50 demos × 3 seeds, plus 100 / 200 (1 seed, directional).
  • ID + OOD splits per axis: matched train condition vs. held‑out variant.
  • Observations: 128×128 RGB — external camera + wrist camera.
Slide 4 · 2:00–3:15What Visual Diversity Matters for Pixel BC

Transcript · target 75s

The benchmark has two tasks: direct reaching to a cube, and obstacle-aware reaching, where the arm has to route around a blocking obstacle before reaching the same target. We use 128 by 128 RGB observations from an external camera and a wrist camera. The four visual diversity axes are color, spatial target distribution, camera viewpoint, and lighting direction and intensity. For each axis we define a narrow and a diverse training condition, on each of the two tasks — sixteen train configurations total. We collect demonstration budgets of five, twenty, and fifty with three seeds, plus one hundred and two hundred at seed zero for directional trends. Every train config has matched in-distribution and held-out out-of-distribution evaluation on its visual axis. The total is one hundred forty-four datasets, and the comparison is interpretable because we vary one factor at a time.

Slide 5 · 3:15–4:30What Visual Diversity Matters for Pixel BC

Transcript · target 75s

We compare three visual policies plus one diagnostic. The scratch CNN is a small end-to-end network trained directly on pixels. The frozen ResNet-18 takes the ImageNet pretrained encoder, freezes it, and trains a projection plus policy head. The partial ResNet-18 unfreezes the later residual blocks. All three share the same loss, action space, and rollout horizon. After we saw that visual diversity alone did not solve the obstacle-aware spatial OOD case, we added a diagnostic: same RGB inputs, plus one-hot task stage and explicit obstacle and target geometry. This is privileged input by design. It is not a method we are proposing for deployment. Its job is to identify what information the visual policies failed to extract from pixels alone.

Result one: at budget fifty, with three training seeds, the scratch CNN handles camera and color shifts much better than expected. Out-of-distribution camera success at one centimeter is fifty seven point nine percent. Color is forty one. Lighting is twenty. Spatial distribution is the clear failure: only two point nine percent. This pattern holds across every model family — spatial OOD never escapes single digits, even at budget two hundred. Notice that on spatial, the frozen ResNet actually edges out scratch CNN. The takeaway is not that scratch is uniformly best — it is that spatial shift is uniquely brutal.

Result two is about how we measure success. At budget two hundred, scratch CNN out-of-distribution success looks great at five centimeters: eighty six percent. Result C · model comparison

Pretrained Encoders Are Not a Free Win in Simulation

  • Scratch CNN beats Frozen ResNet‑18 by ~30pp on OOD@1cm.
  • Frozen ResNet ends closer to the target on average (smoother), but less precise.
  • Partial fine‑tuning is unstable across axes — not a clean win over either.
Why: ImageNet features were not built for sim‑image control geometry. Representation quality only helps when it captures what the controller needs.

Result three answers the pretrained-encoder question. Frozen ResNet-18 reaches only thirteen point eight percent precision at one centimeter on out-of-distribution rollouts at budget two hundred — about thirty points below scratch CNN’s forty three point five. Slide 9 · 6:50–8:10What Visual Diversity Matters for Pixel BC

Transcript · target 80s

This is the headline result. After spatial remained weak, we collected an edge-balanced obstacle-aware dataset that covers corners, edges, and interior target regions evenly. Visual-only training still failed: scratch CNN reached only one point three percent at one centimeter on out-of-distribution rollouts. We then ran the diagnostic: same RGB images, same architecture, but with one-hot task stage and explicit target and obstacle geometry as additional inputs. The scratch model jumped to fifty three point three percent precision. Final distance dropped from nineteen point three to four point two centimeters. This is the punchline: the obstacle-aware spatial OOD failure is not a visual diversity problem. The visual encoder was not extracting enough phase and spatial information from pixels alone, even with the same data. The left video is visual-only failure; the right is phase-plus-geometry success at the same target locations.

  • Phase alone fails — doesn’t locate target or obstacle. Geometry carries the load. Phase compounds it.

Transcript · target 40s

The ablation makes the diagnostic defensible. Phase alone fails at zero percent — knowing the task stage doesn’t tell you where the target or obstacle is. Target geometry alone gives seven percent. Full geometry — target plus obstacle — gives twenty one. Phase plus geometry together gives fifty three. So geometry does most of the work, and the phase signal compounds it. Bucket-level: corner targets jump from zero to eighty two percent. This is information, not appearance.
